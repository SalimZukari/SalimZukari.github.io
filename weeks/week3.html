The Hook

Spider-Man is exactly as central as his 106 links predict

All numbers below are on the undirected giant component (277 characters, 1,421 links) unless stated otherwise, and every z-score is against 200 degree-preserving shuffles of that same component. Degree answers "how popular"; betweenness answers "how much does information flow through you"; closeness answers "how quickly do you reach everyone."

Both z-scores sit within about one standard deviation of the null: a character with 106 links looks exactly like this in any network with this degree sequence, real or shuffled. Spider-Man isn't an unusually well-positioned bridge — he's just enormous, and enormous nodes behave predictably.

The Real Brokers

Who's more (or less) of a bridge than their degree predicts?

Betweenness z-score (200 degree-preserving shuffles) plotted against degree, undirected giant component. Points above zero are bigger bridges than their degree alone predicts; points below are well-connected but not brokers.

Hercules and Black Widow connect the core Avengers/Hulk/Wolverine hub cluster to low-degree peripheries the hub core doesn't touch directly — Hercules to a scattering of cosmic/Asgardian characters, Black Widow to a Spider-Man-adjacent street-level fringe. Rockman is the single most surprising node in the whole component: degree 2, but one of his two neighbors (The Witness) has no other route into the giant component at all, so every shortest path through that dead end runs through Rockman. Black Cat is the mirror image: more links than either broker, but her connections stay inside one already-dense neighborhood — well-connected, not a broker.

Why is closeness harder to be surprising at?
Closeness is a sum over every other node's distance, so it self-averages — one unusual edge only nudges a small fraction of those terms. Betweenness is a sum over specific shortest paths, so one structurally load-bearing edge (like Rockman's dead-end neighbor) can dominate it completely. Neither Hercules nor Black Widow show up near either end of the closeness ranking.
Break the Universe

Which removal fragments the network the most?

Giant-component fraction vs. characters removed, five orders. Click a legend chip to show or hide a line. "Betweenness (dynamic)" recomputes betweenness after every single removal; every other order is fixed at the start.

Removing any one character barely dents a 277-node component — even Spider-Man only knocks it down 2%. Real fragmentation is a multi-removal story, and the order matters: recomputing betweenness after every removal beats every fixed-order strategy, including PageRank, and every targeted order fragments the network far faster than random removal — robust to random loss, fragile to targeted attack.

Six Degrees of Spider-Man

How far is everyone, really?

Spider-Man is the giant component's center (radius 3) — so by definition, nobody in his own component is more than 3 hops from him. Type a character below, or toggle "follow arrows" to search along directed Wikipedia links instead (does the character's own article eventually link its way to Spider-Man's?).

Direction flips the story: Spider-Man's own article links out to only 9 other characters, so following arrows outward from him reaches a small, specific slice of the network — nowhere near everyone. Flip it around and ask who has any directed chain leading into him, and the vast majority do, since so many articles link to him somewhere along the way even when he never links back.

Cliques & Team Homophily

The biggest cliques, named — are they all teams?

Every maximal 8-clique here shares the same five-character X-Men core, with a few more X-Men-adjacent teammates rotating through the remaining seats — yes, they're all teams. That's suggestive but not proof of homophily by itself (cliques could just be a byproduct of degree), so we tested directly against Wikidata team labels.

Why a label-shuffle null, not the usual edge shuffle?
Degree assortativity asks "is this surprising given the degree sequence" — the right question when degree is a known driver. Team membership isn't a structural graph property at all; it's metadata draped over fixed nodes. The right null re-deals the labels across the same nodes and leaves every edge untouched, isolating exactly the question being asked.

What We Can and Can't Conclude

Limits, and the bottom line

This is a Wikipedia hyperlink graph, not a social network — every centrality here measures editorial/narrative prominence, not in-universe importance. The degree-preserving shuffle tests whether a result needs more than the degree sequence to appear; it does not rule out every other explanation. A z-score of +4 for Hercules means "not explained by degree alone," not "definitely explained by his crossover history" — though that's the best reading we have from his neighbor list. Team homophily's z ≈ 17.5 is tested against a completely different null (label-shuffle, not edge-shuffle) and shouldn't be read on the same scale as the degree-assortativity z. The removal experiment operates on a static snapshot and says nothing about how the network might be rewired after a real disruption.

Conclusion. Spider-Man is exactly as central as his 106 links predict — a sanity check, not a finding. The real brokers are Hercules and Black Widow, and far more extremely, Rockman. Fragmenting the network seriously is a multi-removal story where the order matters — dynamically recomputed betweenness beats every other strategy tried here. And despite the "six degrees" framing, everybody in Spider-Man's own giant component is at most three hops from him, as long as you don't insist the links go the right way.
